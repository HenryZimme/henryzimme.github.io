Strategic Defense Dogma

By Henry Zimmerman

Reagan's 'Star Wars' Speech and the Politics of Missile Defense

On March 23, 1983, President Reagan began the final stretch of his address on military funding increases with an unexpected segue that caught even his own national security advisors off guard.1 He declared: “the human spirit must be capable of rising above dealing with other nations and human beings by threatening their existence.”2 Known as ‘The Great Communicator,’ Reagan used this evocative turn of phrase to announce the start of a new nuclear defense program termed the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), likening the effort to that of the Manhattan Project. This new program would mark a profound shift in U.S. military strategy, rejecting the doctrine of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD) for the defensive guarantees of a system based on innovations not feasible before the end of the century.3 Ultimately, Reagan’s announcement of the Strategic Defense Initiative prioritized political considerations that clashed with the strategic calculus of national security, resulting in a program disconnected from the operational reality of missile defense.

While it concerned supposedly bipartisan issues of national security, the circumstances and contents of Reagan’s SDI speech indicate that it served Reagan's political purposes. After losing 26 House seats in the 1982 midterm elections, Reagan was far from the commanding victory he eventually won in the 1984 election.4 In March 1983, 18 months out from the election, his approval rating was 41%.5 The Republican Party’s 1980 platform rejected offensive measures as the only form of nuclear deterrence, advocating for a “vigorous research and development of an effective anti-ballistic missile system” to match one (allegedly) “already at hand in the Soviet Union.”6 Lobbyists from the hawkishly conservative advocacy group High Frontier, led by Lt. General Daniel O. Graham, supported this agenda, publishing the report titled High Frontier: A New National Strategy, which served as inspiration for Reagan’s speech.7 While his approval ratings did not change immediately after the speech, initiating the SDI had the potential to change his political fortunes for the better by carrying out his stated partisan agenda.8

Even though Reagan may have benefited politically by following through on this campaign promise, the signaling value of the imagery Reagan provided perhaps was even more valuable for his campaign. In their psychological analysis of President Reagan’s public image, Thomas and Baas note that Reagan’s image perhaps both carried more weight than his policies in the eyes of voters and affected how these policies were viewed in themselves. Indeed, throughout his presidency, Reagan’s personal approval rating consistently exceeded that of his most pertinent policies.9 His Strategic Defense Initiative speech, though, allowed him to use his ethos and pathos to shape public perception of missile defense strategy by commenting on the human condition. Making eye contact with the television camera, Reagan asks, “Wouldn't it be better to save lives than to avenge them? Are we not capable of demonstrating our peaceful intentions by applying all our abilities and our ingenuity to achieving a truly lasting stability?”10 His answer is an emphatic “We must.” Tapping into nuclear anxieties heightened by a strengthened Soviet ballistic missile program, Reagan notes, “We seek neither military superiority nor political advantage. Our only purpose — one all people share — is to search for ways to reduce the danger of nuclear war.”11 Beyond discussing the ethical implications of the strategy of deterrence, Reagan’s ability to clearly communicate his moral conscience in contexts of national security signaled his virtue to the American Public, easing the process of personal identification identified by Thomas and Baas and thus bolstering his electoral appeal.

Nonetheless, Reagan’s moral clarity lacked a counterpart in strategic and operational clarity. Drafting his speech only five days before his oration on television, Reagan did not consult Secretary of State George Shultz or Secretary of Defense Caspar Weinberger before the speech aired.12 Accordingly, the speech framed SDI in a way that upset NATO allies and required acrobatics from Weinberger in the Harvard International Review to explain the strategic viability of the SDI, both appealing to and challenging the ‘conventional thinking’ of the MAD consensus.13 While Reagan described an SDI that would “render… these nuclear weapons impotent and obsolete,” the strategically permissible version of the program identified in the Hoffman report was much limited in scope: instead of a space-based defensive system that would intercept all Soviet ICBMs shortly after launch as envisioned by Reagan, the report instead advocated for a limited missile defense system that would harden military targets such as silos against nuclear strikes.14 It noted that even a 99% effective system would not achieve the goal of nuclear obsolescence; a few large warheads could still inflict unacceptable levels of destruction.15 Further, a 99% intercept rate was exceedingly optimistic given U.S. capabilities circa 1983: the Hoffman report notes that an uncertain 10 or 20-year timeline would be required just to develop the prerequisite technologies for an effective system, without considering the time it would take to deploy such a system at a scale and redundancy that could achieve Reagan’s goal of nuclear obsolescence.16 In a Senate Committee on Foreign Relations session on Strategic Defense and Antisatellite Weapons, Democratic Senator from Ohio and Mercury astronaut John Glenn notes that Reagan “far oversold” the SDI, starting it “for political reasons.” In a nod to the SDI’s moniker “Star Wars,” Glenn references another science fiction movie in comparison, “If [Reagan] wants to appear to be Buck Rogers … I appreciate that. But I just cannot see us going through all this business of deployment, having great teams going out, doing decision making and all of that business when the basic system has not even been invented.”17 Further, costs of such systems would be much greater than the costs of offensive missiles aimed at the U.S., creating economically asymmetrical warfare conditions that would not provide significant strategic advantage.18

Glenn’s skepticism was largely vindicated: Soviet archives indicate the SDI did not hasten the fall of the USSR, and by 1988, the ~12 billion dollars of R&D spending produced little more for most Americans than interesting “Science and Society” type questions for physics teachers to ask students.19 However, Reagan won a commanding re-election in 1984 with 58.8% of the popular vote, potentially vindicating his political strategy even if not endorsing his geopolitical one.20 Rather, Reagan’s SDI speech and the resulting program showed that the political utility of national security policies can be incommensurable with their strategic value.
